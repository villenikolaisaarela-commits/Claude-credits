On the coast of Inkoo, among old oak trees, stands a timber house in two gabled volumes. It was placed between the trees so that not a single oak had to be felled.

Starting point

The site is a slope of about 4,000 square metres running down to the sea. Thirteen old oaks and a few pines grow on it. The family wanted a house to live in all year round, with a view of the sea even in winter, when the trees are bare.

The roots of an oak reach further than its crown. We mapped the trees with an arborist before the first sketch, and the root zones left two narrow strips where a building could go.

Two volumes

The house is split into two gabled volumes that sit on those strips. The taller one holds the kitchen, dining area and a living room open to the ridge. The lower one holds the bedrooms, bathroom and sauna. Between them is an entrance hall that looks out to both the garden and the woods.

The gable end of the living room has a sliding door 3.6 metres wide. In summer it opens onto a deck that wraps around the house and follows the level of the ground.

Materials

The facade is thermally modified pine fixed vertically, with no corner or window trims. Within a few years the boards will turn the same grey as the oak trunks. Downpipes and window flashings are galvanised steel, left to dull.

Inside, the walls are white and the floors are pale oak boards. The sauna benches and panelling are aspen.

The view in winter

The living-room windows are placed so that the bay shows between the bare trees. The sills are kept low, so the sea is visible from the sofa too.

Carbon footprint

No climate declaration was required when the house was permitted, but we calculated the carbon footprint using the Ministry of the Environment’s method. The largest savings came from the screw piles, which avoided a concrete foundation, and from the wood-fibre insulation.

Key figures

  • 13oaks kept
  • 11.6kg CO₂e/m²/yr, carbon footprint
  • 38 tCO₂ stored in the timber

Carbon footprint calculated with the Finnish Ministry of the Environment’s method over 50 years. Carbon storage estimated from the volume of timber in the frame, insulation and facade.
